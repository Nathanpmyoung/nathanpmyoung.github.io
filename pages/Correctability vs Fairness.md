- [[Blog]]
- I care a lot about improving. 
- A key way of improving is being able to be corrected. 
- Recently, I've noticed this to be in conflict with fairness. 
- In a group chat, somebody was a bit snappy to me and I snapped back. A friend pointed this out and told me that my comment was unnecessary, which it was. 
- Now this friend doesn't always take this kind of advice particularly well. I don't tend to give him advice for this reason. 
- So I have an opportunity to improve as a person. But it wouldn't necessarily happen the other way round. 
- And some part of me feels this is unfair. How dare he tell me what to do when he wouldn't listen to me? Why should I listen to him. I am gonna make a rude comment. Screw him.
- This response seems childish, but sometimes in the moment I can be quite childish. It was quite childish to want to respond rudely in the first place. In the moment, it's easy for the blood to rush in my ears and for me to want to say or do whatever my emotions demand. 
- This conflict also exists in apologies. To apologise is to be correctable, to acknowledge that one ought to have done differently. But many times I have apologised to people who I do not think will apologise to me back, and in fact, it might seem like I'm acknowledging fault in a way that they just will never do. 
- But really, for me, there's a question of whether it's more important to have perfect fairness or for me to improve. 
- In general, I think my improvement matters more. I want to encourage people to correct me even if they wouldn't take correction from me. I want to acknowledge error even to people who wouldn't acknowledge error. The question is whether I can improve as a person, not about fairness I think. 
- It seems a common failure mode for people to get stuck on how correction is delivered or by whom. When really, most of us have many ways in which we can improve. This might not be true for high-ranking politicians or CEOs who are receiving correction all the time from many angles. Though I think many such people could take more correction. But I understand that for them, exactly how they hear that correction and make ensuring that correction is high quality may be more important. But for most of us, I think we could do to receive more corrections. Certainly I could. 
- And it seems a common pattern that people will say, "Oh, I love correction," but when correction actually comes, they subtly make it clear that they don't want to receive it. 
- I see there's a bit in Rationalism where some respected rationalists will say they want to be corrected, but in practice, engage anybody attempting to correct them in a long argument, which is a surefire way to ensure that nobody does. 
- Likewise, when I was a Christian, there seemed many things where questions weren't welcomed. In a sermon on miracles it was explicily fine to ask questions about what actually happened, but implicity, it was not. Questions about the specific timing and details of the miracle and who could corroborate found a slightly chilly reception. They weren't not explicitly banned, not at all, but implicitly, people wouldn't be grateful for it. And I think that led to an environment where much questioning didn't happen. 
- Here are some ways I try to live:
	I try to be gracious in accepting correction. I do not always live up to this standard, and I'm sorry if you've tried to give me feedback and I have been ungracious about it. I'd appreciate hearing about when on my anonymous feedback form. At least one such person has done so, thank you.
	If somebody corrects me, I think about whether they're right or not first of all.
	If someone corrects me who I don't like, or in a manner I find frustrating, I try to imagine the correction came from the earth itself. What would I think?
	If I am wrong, I try and apologise. 
	If I wrong someone I don't like or get on with I still try to apologise first.
	If I sense they have wronged me much more than have wronged them, then I can apologize and just cease interacting with them if I wish. But I think the apology is more about me than it is about them.
	I try to be curious. One big recent lesson for me is that sometimes it's good to listen without theories. Just to hear and think. Often if I upset someone I have very little understanding of why.
	I have an [admonymous page](https://www.admonymous.co/nathanpmyoung), where people can leave me anonymous feedback. I've found it useful over the years.
	I try to read my anonymous feedback when I'm in a good and receptive mood because it can be pretty hard to hear. 
- Over the years, I've been surprised by how much behavior like this has sometimes surprised or impacted people. As the saying goes, "You catch more flies with honey than with vinegar." Several times, people I haven't talked to for years have eventually become friends again because I didn't create an environment of animosity and was actually happy to acknowledge when I had made errors. I think it's much easier for somebody to come back and perhaps even ignore the error themselves if it can start by me saying, "Yeah, I don't think this is all one-sided. I've got some things to apologize for."
